Probability distributions: from real counts to useful models

Start with actual orders, then explore how Normal, Binomial, and Poisson distributions describe uncertainty—and how to choose one for a business question.

What is a probability distribution?

A probability distribution is a map from possible outcomes to their probabilities. It tells us what values a random variable can take and how likely each value is.

Think of it as a forecast of variation. It does not usually tell you exactly what will happen next. It describes the range of outcomes and how probability is spread across that range.

Observed frequency becomes probability

Show the arithmetic

P(X = 2) = days with 2 orders ÷ all observed days
= 3 ÷ 10 = 0.30 = 30%
P(X ≤ 2) = days with 0, 1, or 2 orders ÷ 10
= (1 + 1 + 3) ÷ 10 = 0.50 = 50%

The first is an exact-outcome probability. The second adds probabilities for all outcomes up to 2. This is the core idea behind a CDF.

Dataset limitation: 10 days is a tiny sample. The estimated 30% for exactly 2 orders is useful for intuition, but another 10-day sample could look different. A probability model can help smooth out this sampling noise and reason about outcomes not yet observed.
Two ways to ask about probability

PMF/PDF and CDF: exact value versus accumulated probability

Discrete outcomes: PMF

For counts such as orders or defects, outcomes are separate whole numbers. The probability mass function (PMF) gives the probability of each exact value.

PMF: p(x) = P(X = x)

For the 10-day sample, p(3) = 3/10 = 0.30, because 3 orders occurred on 3 of the 10 days.

Continuous outcomes: PDF

For measurements such as delivery time, values can be any number in a range. A probability density function (PDF) describes density, not the probability of one exact point.

P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = area under the PDF from a to b

For a continuous variable, the probability of exactly one value is zero: P(X = 4.000...) = 0. A meaningful probability covers an interval, like delivery taking between 3 and 5 days.

Cumulative distribution function

The CDF adds probability from the far left up to a chosen value:

F(x) = P(X ≤ x)

For discrete orders, F(2) = P(X ≤ 2). In our dataset that is 5/10 = 50%.

For a continuous distribution, the CDF is the area under the PDF to the left of x. To find a range probability:

P(a < X ≤ b) = F(b) − F(a)

A useful mental picture: the PDF shows the shape of probability; the CDF is the running total as you move left to right.

Continuous distribution

Normal distribution: the bell-shaped model

The Normal distribution is a symmetric, continuous distribution centered at its mean. It is useful for quantities influenced by many small effects, such as measurement error, some delivery-time metrics, and dimensions.

f(x) = [1 ÷ (σ√(2π))] × e−(x−μ)² ÷ (2σ²) μ = mean (center); σ = standard deviation (spread); variance = σ². The curve’s total area is 1.
Important: PDF height is density, not a probability. Probability is area over an interval. The curve’s height changes when the parameters change, but total area stays 1.

Why does the shape change?

  • Change μ: the whole curve shifts left or right. The mean changes the center, not the basic shape.
  • Increase variance: values are more spread out. The curve becomes wider and lower so its total area remains 1.
  • Decrease variance: values cluster more tightly near the mean, making the curve narrower and taller.

Discrete distribution

Binomial distribution: count successes in a fixed number of tries

Use a Binomial model when there are a fixed number of trials, each trial has two outcomes (success/failure), the success probability stays the same, and trials are independent.

X ~ Binomial(n, p)
P(X = k) = C(n,k) pk(1−p)n−k n = number of trials; p = success probability per trial; k = successes. C(n,k) counts how many ways the k successes can occur.
Business example: 10 customers independently receive the same promotion. Each has a 20% chance to purchase. What is the probability exactly 3 purchase?

The CDF adds PMF values from 0 through k. Use it for questions like “at most 3 purchases” or “no more than 3 defects.”

Why does the Binomial shape change?

  • Increasing n increases the possible range from 0 to n. It also increases the expected count np.
  • Changing p shifts the likely counts: small p favors counts near 0; large p favors counts near n.
  • When p is near 0.5, the distribution tends to be more symmetric. Near 0 or 1, it tends to lean toward one end.
  • Spread is measured by np(1−p), so it depends on both the number of trials and success chance.
Discrete distribution

Poisson distribution: count events in a fixed interval

Use a Poisson model for counts of events in a specified time, area, distance, or volume when events occur independently at a roughly stable average rate.

X ~ Poisson(λ)
P(X = k) = e−λ λk ÷ k! λ is the expected number of events in the interval. It is both the mean and the variance.
Business example: a support team receives an average of 4 urgent tickets per hour. What is the probability of exactly 2 tickets in the next hour?

The Poisson count range starts at 0 and has no fixed maximum. In practice, almost all probability is concentrated within a manageable range around λ.

Why does the Poisson shape change?

  • λ is the average count and the center of the distribution. Raising λ shifts typical outcomes to larger counts.
  • Variance is also λ. A larger event rate means more absolute variation, so the distribution spreads out.
  • At small λ, counts cannot go below zero, so the shape is strongly right-skewed. At larger λ, it becomes more symmetric.
  • The interval matters: a rate of 4 per hour corresponds to an expected 2 per half-hour, assuming a stable rate.
Applied statistics

Choosing the right distribution for a business problem

Start with the outcome and the process that generates it. The context tells you which model assumptions are reasonable.

Quick decision guide

Business questionCandidate modelWhy
What share of 20 contacted leads convert?BinomialFixed number of yes/no trials
How many support tickets arrive this hour?PoissonCount events in a fixed interval
How variable are package weights?NormalContinuous measurement, often roughly symmetric
What is the chance delivery takes between 2 and 4 days?Normal, if justifiedContinuous interval probability from area/CDF

Choose a scenario

Ask these questions before choosing

  1. What is the variable? A count, a yes/no outcome, or a continuous measurement?
  2. Is the number of trials fixed? If yes, and each trial is independent with the same success chance, consider Binomial.
  3. Are you counting events across a time/space interval? If the average rate is stable and events are independent, consider Poisson.
  4. Is it a continuous measurement with a roughly symmetric, bell-like pattern? A Normal model may be useful; check data and context rather than assuming.
  5. Do the assumptions fit? Strong seasonality, changing success probabilities, clustering, hard limits, or skew can make these models a poor fit.
Model, not magic: a distribution is a simplifying description. Use historical data, check assumptions, and compare predictions with actual outcomes before relying on it for staffing, inventory, or service promises.
